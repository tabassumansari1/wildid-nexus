In the field

Three moves, then a report.

  1. 01

    Attach the sightings

    Several photos, a clip, or both. Patrol camera, phone, trap — mixed is fine.

  2. 02

    Read the coat

    Each file becomes marking points. Clips contribute their sharpest frames, not a blur of motion.

  3. 03

    Keep only geometry

    Points must agree under one transform. If they do, it is the same animal. If they cannot, it is not. If the frame is too poor, WILD-ID Nexus says so.

What is actually compared

Not the smile. Not the stance. The pattern that does not repeat.

Every adult of a species is built on the same skeleton. Mouth movement and body shape will not separate yesterday’s tiger from today’s. Scars, ear nicks, stripes and spots will.

  • Locate. The animal region is separated from the waterhole and the trees, so the background cannot fake a match.
  • Describe. SIFT marking points, the same family of local features HotSpotter used for zebras, giraffes and whales.
  • Verify. A match is kept only when those points fit a single geometric transform. Lookalike spots that do not line up are thrown out.
  • Group. Files that pass are one individual. The report shows geometric evidence and marking-point correspondences, not a probability score.

Field note

It will refuse to guess.

A soft, dark, or edge-on frame does not get called “a different animal” just to sound decisive. That result stays inconclusive. Published wildlife re-identification is strong on patterned coats in clear light, and honestly weaker when the marking is hidden. The report shows the correspondences so you can see the reason, not just a badge.
